# How Debt Accounts Work

Debt accounts (also called liability accounts) track money you owe to others. This includes credit cards, loans, and any other obligations.

## Why Debt Math Seems "Backwards"

If you're used to thinking about asset accounts, debt accounts can feel counterintuitive at first. Here's the key difference:

| Balance Type | What It Means |
| --- | --- |
| **Positive balance** | You owe money |
| **Negative balance** | The lender owes you money (overpayment, refund) |

And transactions work in the opposite direction from assets:

| Action | Asset Account | Debt Account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spend money | Balance decreases | Balance increases (more debt) |
| Receive money | Balance increases | Balance decreases (less debt) |

> [!TIP]
> Think of a debt balance as "Amount Owed" rather than "money I have." A higher number means you owe more, not that you have more.

## Credit Card Example

Let's walk through a typical credit card scenario:

| Action | Balance Change | New Balance | What It Means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting balance | — | $500 | You owe $500 |
| Buy groceries (+$80) | +$80 | $580 | You now owe $580 |
| Gas purchase (+$45) | +$45 | $625 | You now owe $625 |
| Make payment (-$300) | -$300 | $325 | You now owe $325 |

Notice how spending **increases** the balance (you owe more) and payments **decrease** it (you owe less).

### Overpayment Scenario

What happens if you pay more than you owe?

| Action | Balance Change | New Balance | What It Means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting balance | — | $100 | You owe $100 |
| Make payment (-$150) | -$150 | -$50 | Card company owes you $50 |
| Buy coffee (+$5) | +$5 | -$45 | Card company owes you $45 |

A negative balance on a credit card means you've overpaid—the credit card company owes *you* money. This might happen from:

- Overpayment
- Refunds for returned purchases
- Statement credits or rewards

## Types of Debt Accounts

Sure supports three types of liability accounts:

### Credit Card

Track your credit card balances and spending. Credit card accounts can include:

| Field | Description |
| --- | --- |
| APR | Annual percentage rate (interest rate) |
| Available Credit | Remaining credit you can use |
| Minimum Payment | Minimum amount due |
| Annual Fee | Yearly card fee |
| Expiration Date | Card expiration date |

### Loan

Track mortgages, auto loans, student loans, and other installment debt.

| Subtype | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Mortgage | Home loan |
| Student | Student loan debt |
| Auto | Car loan |
| Other | Personal loans, etc. |

Loan accounts support additional fields:

| Field | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Interest Rate | Annual interest rate |
| Rate Type | Fixed, variable, or adjustable |
| Term | Loan duration (months) |
| Start date | Date the loan was drawn down (optional — defaults to the account's opening balance date) |
| Down payment | What you paid upfront. Not part of the amortization, but used to calculate your leverage ratio on the overview. |
| Insurance rate | Annual insurance premium rate, charged monthly alongside the repayment. |
| Insurance type | How the premium is calculated: **Level term** charges the rate on the original balance each month (constant premium); **Decreasing** charges it on the outstanding balance (falls as you repay). |
| Monthly Payment | Calculated from loan details (fixed-rate only) |

> [!NOTE]
> Sure calculates a monthly payment automatically for fixed-rate loans. For variable-rate and adjustable-rate loans, use the Schedule tab to see the current payment amount.

### Other Liability

For debts that don't fit the above categories:

- Personal loans from friends/family
- Medical bills
- IOUs
- Tax obligations

## How Debt Affects Net Worth

Liabilities **reduce** your net worth:

```
Net Worth = Total Assets - Total Liabilities
```

When you pay down debt, your net worth increases (even though your cash decreases). This is because the debt reduction outweighs the cash spent—you're converting a liquid asset (cash) into reduced liability (less debt).

### Example: Paying Off a Credit Card

| Account | Before Payment | After $500 Payment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Checking (Asset) | $2,000 | $1,500 |
| Credit Card (Liability) | $500 | $0 |
| **Net Worth** | **$1,500** | **$1,500** |

Your net worth stays the same because you traded $500 of cash for $500 of debt reduction. But now you have no credit card debt!

## Adding Debt Accounts

To add a debt account:

1. Click **+ Add Account** on your dashboard
2. Select the liability type (Credit Card, Loan, or Other Liability)
3. Enter your account details:
   - Account name
   - Current balance (the amount you currently owe)
   - For loans: original balance (the principal when the loan started), interest rate, term, and rate type
4. Click **Create Account**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Make sure to select a liability account type (Credit Card, Loan, or Other Liability) when adding debt. If you accidentally create a Cash account for your credit card, the balance calculations will be incorrect.

### Loans with a different opening and current balance

When you add a loan, you can enter both the original principal (opening balance) and your current remaining balance. These two figures are often different — for example, a mortgage you took out years ago has a much lower remaining balance today than when it started.

Sure preserves both values: the original principal is recorded as the opening balance on the loan's start date, and your current remaining balance is anchored to today. The first sync uses your current balance as the baseline and calculates forward from there, so the balance you entered is never overwritten.

## Understanding Loan Payments

When you make a loan payment, the payment typically covers:

1. **Interest** - Cost of borrowing
2. **Principal** - Reducing the amount you owe

Sure tracks your loan balance, which represents the remaining principal. As you make payments, the balance decreases until the loan is paid off.

> [!TIP]
> To track loan payments accurately, create a [transfer](./transfers.mdx) from your checking account to your loan account. This properly reflects the payment in both accounts.

## Loan overview

The **Overview** tab on a loan account shows a grid of summary cards and a repayment progress section.

### Summary cards

| Card | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Original Loan Amount | The original principal (what was borrowed) |
| Remaining Loan Amount | Your current outstanding balance |
| Interest Rate | The rate in force today — for variable-rate loans this reflects the most recent recorded rate change |
| Monthly Payment | The contracted payment for fixed-rate loans; for variable-rate loans, the payment currently in force based on the latest re-amortization |
| Term | Total loan duration in years (or months for loans shorter than one year) |
| Original Payoff Date | The date the final scheduled payment falls due under the original contract |
| Type | Fixed, variable, or adjustable |
| Total Cost | Total principal plus interest over the life of the loan. When an insurance rate is recorded the card is labeled **Total Cost incl. Insurance** and includes the premium total. |
| Insurance | Only shown when an insurance rate is recorded. Displays the total premium if a schedule is available, or the annual rate if not. |
| Leverage | Only shown when a down payment is recorded. Displays the loan-to-deposit ratio and a band (Conservative, Moderate, or High). |

### Repayment progress

Below the summary cards, the overview shows how far into repayment you are:

- **Repaid ring** — A donut chart showing the percentage of the original loan amount already repaid, based on your current balance.
- **Payment breakdown** — The current scheduled installment split into principal, interest, and insurance (if applicable), each shown as an amount and a share of the total payment.

## Amortization schedule

Sure generates a full amortization schedule for any loan that has an interest rate, a term, and an original balance — including variable-rate and adjustable-rate loans. The schedule uses a constant-payment (French) method: interest is charged on the outstanding principal each month, and the remainder of each payment reduces principal. This matches the table your lender uses.

To view the schedule, open a loan account and select the **Schedule** tab.

The tab shows summary figures at the top:

| Figure | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Opening payment | The level amount at the start of the loan |
| Total interest | All interest charged over the life of the loan |
| Total cost | Principal plus total interest |
| Payoff date | The date of the final scheduled payment |

For fixed-rate loans, the opening payment is also the contracted monthly payment for the life of the loan. For variable-rate and adjustable-rate loans, Sure re-amortises the schedule at each recorded rate change, so the payment amount can change after a rate change takes effect. The summary labels this as the opening payment rather than "the" monthly payment when the schedule re-amortises.

Below the summary is a full payment table with one row per period:

| Column | Description |
| --- | --- |
| # | Payment number |
| Date | Scheduled payment date |
| Payment | Total amount due that period |
| Principal | Portion applied to the outstanding balance |
| Interest | Portion charged as interest |
| Remaining balance | Outstanding principal after the payment |

Rows for payments whose scheduled date has already passed are shaded to distinguish elapsed periods from upcoming ones.

The monthly payment shown on the loan overview is read directly from the schedule, so it is cent-accurate. For example, a $500,000 mortgage at 3.5% over 360 months shows $2,245.22 rather than a rounded figure.

### Variable-rate loans

For variable-rate and adjustable-rate loans, you can record rate changes directly on the loan. Each change requires an effective date and the new annual rate. Sure re-amortises the schedule from the point of each change: the payment for periods after the effective date is resized to pay off the remaining balance over the remaining term at the new rate.

The interest charged in a period always uses the rate that was in force when the period opened, so a change effective on a payment date takes effect from that payment forward — it does not retroactively change the interest already accrued in the current month.

A variable-rate loan with no recorded rate changes runs at its base rate, just as a fixed-rate loan does.

> [!NOTE]
> The monthly payment field on the loan overview is only shown for fixed-rate loans. For variable-rate loans, use the Schedule tab to see the current payment in force.

## Loan balance chart

Loan accounts include a balance chart that shows three series together:

| Series | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Actual (solid) | Your recorded balance from origination to today |
| Scheduled (dashed) | The original contract balance from origination to the contracted payoff date |
| Projected (dashed) | Where today's recorded balance is heading if you keep paying the contracted amount |

The gap between the actual and scheduled lines shows whether you are ahead of or behind the original contract. The projected line starts at today's balance and ends when the loan is paid off at the contracted repayment.

The chart offers a period picker with the same windows available on other account charts (month, 90 days, year, 5 years, 10 years, and all time). Each window is clamped so it never opens before the loan's origination date. Under **All**, the domain extends far enough to show every series, including the full projected payoff.

The forward-looking series (scheduled and projected) are only drawn under the **All** window, since they extend past today. The summary cards beside the chart show the projection figures regardless of which period is selected.

### Tooltip

Hovering over or keyboard-stepping through the chart shows a tooltip for the nearest scheduled payment date. For each scheduled payment point, the tooltip shows the remaining balance and the breakdown of that payment into principal and interest, using the same labels as the Schedule tab. The opening point (the original loan amount) carries no breakdown because it is not a payment. The actual and projected series show only the balance.

## Payoff projection

The **Schedule** tab shows a payoff projection alongside the amortization table. The projection starts from your current recorded balance and applies the contracted repayment each period to show where the loan is actually heading — not where the original contract said it would be.

The Schedule tab summary shows:

| Figure | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Opening payment | The level amount at the start of the loan |
| Total interest | All interest charged over the life of the loan |
| Total cost | Principal plus total interest |
| Forecasted payoff date | The date the loan will actually be paid off based on today's balance and the contracted repayment |

The forecasted payoff date reflects any extra payments you have already made: if your current balance is lower than the contract expected, the projection finishes sooner and saves interest. If your balance has grown past what the contract expected, the projection may not converge — in that case the tab shows a notice instead of a date.

The projection runs only to the original contracted maturity date. It does not extend the term or invent a new repayment size. This means:

- A borrower who is ahead of schedule finishes earlier and saves interest.
- A borrower whose balance has grown may reach the end of the contracted term still owing a balance. The tab shows that remaining balance rather than a payoff date.

> [!NOTE]
> The projection is not shown when the loan has no amortization schedule (for example, if the interest rate, term, or original balance is missing), or when the current balance is zero.
